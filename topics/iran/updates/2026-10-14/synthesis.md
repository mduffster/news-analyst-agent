## Houthis Strike Aramco Headquarters — CEO Office Damaged

The most consequential Houthi attack of the conflict to date hit the symbolic heart of Saudi Arabia's energy sector.

Oil giant Saudi Aramco's headquarters in Dhahran suffered extensive damage in an attack on Tuesday, with a fire spreading across several floors, three people with direct knowledge of the matter told Reuters. Two regional officials say offices belonging to senior executives with Saudi oil giant Aramco were hit in an overnight Houthi strike on the company's headquarters. One official said the office of the company's chief executive was among those hit. There were no casualties.

The Dhahran strike is the first reported hit on an Aramco corporate site in this round of attacks, and it hit a command center, not a producing or export asset. A company source told Reuters a ballistic missile struck the complex and damaged the top three floors of an office tower. Dhahran houses a major operations center that oversees Aramco's onshore and offshore rigs, pipelines, refineries and export terminals.

Saudi authorities sought to downplay the incident. A limited fire erupted at Aramco's office complex in Dhahran and there were no casualties, Saudi authorities said. A missile attack on an administrative building of Saudi Arabia's state energy company Aramco hit the historic centre of the kingdom's oil industry, sparking a fire that authorities said caused no casualties. The strike by the Yemeni Houthi rebels hit a complex that serves as the nerve centre of the world's largest oil exporter, housing key research and development site, energy operations and thousands of employees and their families.

Residents near the complex reported the intensity of the strike. "This was the loudest explosion I have ever heard in my life," one woman told The National.

**Why this matters:** The Houthis executed their threatened strike on Aramco within 24 hours of issuing warnings. Hitting the CEO's office demonstrates precision strike capability and sends a message that Saudi energy leadership is personally at risk. This is qualitatively different from attacks on airports or refineries—it targets the decision-making center of the world's largest oil company. While production infrastructure remains intact, the symbolic and strategic implications are profound.

## Container Ship Sinks in Hormuz — Iran Claims Mine Warfare

A Singapore-flagged vessel became only the second ship lost since the war began, while Iran for the first time claimed a sinking resulted from mine warfare.

A Singapore-flagged container ship has sunk near the Strait of Hormuz after being hit by a projectile, while a dry bulk carrier damaged in an earlier attack is also reported to be sinking, maritime security sources said on Wednesday. The Singapore-flagged GFS Pride, a small container vessel with capacity for 2,824 twenty-foot equivalent units (TEU), sank while sailing through the strait.

"GFS PRIDE sustained damage and a fire on board, and has since sunk. All 21 crew members were safely evacuated. No injuries have been reported," the Maritime and Port Authority of Singapore (MPA) said in a statement on Wednesday, adding that the vessel had been sailing through the strait on Tuesday.

The loss of the GFS Pride marks the second vessel to sink since the US-Iran war began on February 28, according to data from the UN shipping agency, the International Maritime Organization.

Iran offered a different account. A cargo ship has sunk after hitting a naval mine in the Strait of Hormuz, Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps navy said. It was the first time Iran has reported a ship sinking after hitting a mine during the war. State television quoted a Guard statement saying the ship was traveling on what it called an illegal and dangerous route when it hit the mine Wednesday morning.

Singapore authorities condemned the attacks. "MPA is deeply concerned about the continued attacks on commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz," the Singapore authority said. "Such attacks are unprovoked, unjustifiable, and a breach of international law."

Shipping officials, meanwhile, said two Singapore-registered cargo vessels had been hit by "unknown projectiles" as they went through the strait on Tuesday and Wednesday. One sank, while the other reached safety.

**Analysis:** Whether the sinking resulted from a mine or a projectile, the outcome is the same: Iran has demonstrated it can sink commercial vessels in Hormuz. The mine warfare claim—if accurate—represents a significant escalation, as mines are indiscriminate and difficult to clear. This moves the conflict from targeted harassment to area-denial capability.

## Hormuz Attacks Reach Record Levels; Commercial Transits Near Zero

The pace of attacks on shipping in the Strait of Hormuz reached unprecedented levels, with commercial traffic effectively halted.

An Iranian official told the Reuters news agency that Tehran had started using different offensive tactics against vessels it considered in violation of its restrictions about 10 days ago, adding that this had led to an increase in attacks on such ships. Ships in the Strait of Hormuz have been experiencing the worst spate of attacks since the start of the US and Israel's war on Iran in late February.

According to maritime tracking firm Kpler, 10 tankers were struck in the strait between September 28 and October 4, followed by another strike on October 5. The UK Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) centre has separately recorded 15 other attacks between October 6 and October 14, with 11 of them occurring in just the past four days.

Four vessels were struck on Tuesday and six on Sunday – the highest number of attacks in a single day since the start of the conflict – according to data from security firm Vanguard, the International Maritime Organization (IMO) and the British maritime agency UKMTO. Two seafarers have been reported missing and a container ship reportedly sank as a result of recent attacks.

## IEA: Diesel Supply Crunch to Persist Through 2027

The International Energy Agency's October report delivered a stark warning about fuel supply constraints.

Constraints on the diesel market will extend well into next year, the International Energy Agency warned on Wednesday. In its October Oil Market Report, the IEA said supply would remain tight into the latter half of 2027.

According to the IEA's October Oil Market Report, Singapore diesel prices climbed above $200 a barrel in mid-September, while Europe and the United States saw record highs of roughly $220 a barrel. Retail diesel prices have risen to record levels in most major economies.

Last month saw global diesel exports drop by 700,000 barrels a day year-on-year, according to IEA data. A 1.3 million barrels per day loss from Russia and the Middle East was "only partly offset by higher supply from other sources," the agency said.

Diesel refining margins, the fuel's premium to underlying crude oil, soared to record highs of almost $95 per barrel in September, as reduced refining capacity due to attacks linked to the Iran war slashed production.

World oil demand is forecast to fall by 2.5 mb/d y-o-y in 4Q26 and 2.6 mb/d on average in 2026. As the Iran war manifests itself through restricted product availability, soaring fuel prices and a deteriorating economic outlook, diesel will register 25% of the drop.

**Significance:** The diesel crisis represents the war's most direct impact on global consumers. Record wholesale prices of $220/barrel for diesel translate to elevated costs for transportation, agriculture, and industry worldwide. The IEA's assessment that inventory replenishment won't begin until late 2027 suggests this is now a structural rather than temporary constraint.

## Russia and China Open Diplomatic Channel to Houthis

A new diplomatic development emerged as Moscow and Beijing reached out to the Houthis seeking a ceasefire.

Mohamed al-Bukhaiti, a senior member of the Houthis' political bureau, told AP that Oman had attempted to bring the warring sides back to negotiations, while Russia and China had also pursued contacts through Iran.

"We're welcoming any mediation that leads to an end of the war and the blockade between Sanaa and Riyadh," al-Bukhaiti said. "We're ready for a comprehensive end of the war and the blockade from both sides, not just a temporary ceasefire."

The Houthis nevertheless continued threatening Saudi airports and oil installations.

**Analysis:** This development is significant for several reasons. First, it bypasses the United States entirely, with Russia and China working through Iran to reach the Houthis. Second, the Houthi response—openness to "comprehensive" rather than temporary settlement—suggests they may be seeking an off-ramp while at a position of strength. However, the simultaneous continuation of attacks indicates this is positioning rather than imminent de-escalation. If Moscow and Beijing can broker a Saudi-Houthi agreement, it would demonstrate US irrelevance in this theater while potentially relieving energy price pressure.

## Trump Floats Turkey Intervention; Post-Election Action Signaled

President Trump explored alternative approaches to the Houthi threat while signaling action after November 3.

As the White House weighs further involvement in Saudi Arabia's military campaign against the Houthis, President Donald Trump said Tuesday he "might" ask Turkey to intervene.

Trump told reporters at the White House: "I might do it. President [Recep Tayyip] Erdogan is a great friend of mine. I have a lot of respect for President Erdogan." "President Erdogan, I think, would do as I ask," he added.

However, Turkey has clarified limits to its involvement. Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan ruled out sending Turkish troops abroad to launch attacks under the pact on October 8 while explaining that officials were assessing possible contributions to Saudi Arabia's air defenses.

Turkey, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan signed the Mecca Joint Defense Agreement on August 7, pledging to treat an armed attack on one member as an attack on all. Erdoğan announced Monday that his government was preparing to submit the pact to Turkey's parliament for approval in the coming days. The three countries agreed at an emergency meeting in Riyadh on October 5 to implement their defense commitments and arrange military deployments to Saudi Arabia, subject to each country's laws.

The gesture of solidarity comes after a series of strikes on Riyadh airport killed at least 18 people and wounded more than 300. Earlier this year, Turkey signed a joint defense pact with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan.

**Assessment:** Trump's Turkey gambit would allow escalation against Houthis without direct US involvement before midterms. However, Turkey has explicitly ruled out offensive operations, limiting potential contributions to air defense support. The practical impact may be modest, but the diplomatic signal—bringing NATO's second-largest military into the Saudi coalition—could affect Houthi calculations.

## Continued Attacks on Riyadh; Interception Debris Injures Civilians

Fresh explosions rocked the Saudi capital on Wednesday as the aerial campaign continued.

New explosions have been heard in the Saudi capital, residents said Wednesday, as blame again quickly fell on Iran-backed Houthi rebels in neighboring Yemen. The Saudi-led military coalition fighting the Houthis said it intercepted a ballistic missile fired by the rebels toward Riyadh.

Overnight, Saudi Arabia's Civil Defense said debris from two intercepted Houthi ballistic missiles fell on residential areas in Riyadh, injuring four people including a child and damaging 15 homes.

In the latest attacks, the Saudi-led military coalition fighting the Houthis said it intercepted cruise and ballistic missiles and drones targeting the capital, Riyadh; a ballistic missile targeting Dhahran, which hosts the headquarters of Saudi oil giant Aramco; and a cruise missile targeting Al-Kharj, home to Prince Sultan Air Base and the state-owned Military Industries Corporation.

The attacks continued into Wednesday, in fighting that has killed at least 18 people and injured hundreds more in Saudi Arabia over the past week.
