## 1. Iran Targets Bahrain — First Gulf Basing Attack Since September

The United States military's Central Command (CENTCOM) says it shot down two attack drones launched by Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) on Friday, and that no injuries or damage were caused.

Iran said it targeted a US military installation in Bahrain early Friday in retaliation for what it described as an American attack on Iranian territory, marking its first attempt to strike a military facility in a Gulf state in more than two months.

In a statement posted on the social media platform X, CENTCOM described the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps' (IRGC) claim as false, stating that US forces had shot down both one-way attack drones launched towards Bahrain.

Earlier on Friday, the IRGC claimed that it had targeted and destroyed a US early-warning radar facility in Bahrain in retaliation for an attack on a telecommunications tower in Iran's southern Sirik region.

Al Jazeera's Resul Serdar, reporting from Tehran, said the US has attacked telecommunications networks at military sites in southern Iran "from time to time". "Iran was not responding by attacking US military assets in the region, but now it is the first time they are doing so," he said. "So the question is, will Iran continue to attack US military assets in neighbouring countries, which could lead to another wave of war?"

**Significance:** [VERIFIED] This incident represents a meaningful escalation in Iran's posture. While CENTCOM successfully intercepted the drones, the fact that Iran attempted to strike U.S. basing in Bahrain — home to the U.S. Navy's Fifth Fleet headquarters — after a two-month pause suggests Tehran may be shifting from purely maritime disruption toward more direct confrontation with American forces. The Bahrain attack, combined with Iran's claim of retaliation for U.S. strikes on Iranian telecommunications infrastructure, establishes a new tit-for-tat pattern that increases the risk of rapid escalation cycles.

## 2. Mossad Chief Gofman in Washington — Netanyahu's Direct Push for War Resumption

Roman Gofman, director of the Mossad, has arrived in Washington, DC, to meet with senior officials from the Trump administration to discuss the war with Iran, sources told The Jerusalem Post.

Gofman is meeting with CIA Director John Ratcliffe, the sources said. He is also expected to travel to Miami to meet White House envoy Steve Witkoff, one of the sources said.

The visit by Roman Gofman, one of Benjamin Netanyahu's closest confidants, comes as the Israeli prime minister is pressing President Trump to resume the war against Iran.

Trump has so far rejected pressure from Netanyahu to return to combat. The two discussed the Iran war during a Nov. 5 call, per a U.S. official.

Instead, Trump has favored continuing a naval blockade and economic pressure campaign against Iran while exploring the possibility of a deal.

In the meantime, leaders of the Israeli opposition are concerned Netanyahu will try to launch an attack on Iran — or provoke Iran to attack Israel — in order to undermine Eisenkot's efforts, sources say.

**Significance:** [VERIFIED] Netanyahu is deploying his top intelligence asset to circumvent normal diplomatic channels and press directly for war resumption at the highest levels. The dual-track approach — meeting both CIA Director Ratcliffe and envoy Witkoff — represents a full-court press. However, Trump's continued resistance and his explicit alignment with Gulf states on the "status quo" suggests this lobbying effort is unlikely to succeed in changing U.S. policy.

## 3. IDF Chief Zamir Isolated — Only Voice Against Immediate Strikes

Eyal Zamir has again expressed opposition in recent meetings to actions that Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu wants to carry out against Iran, Channel 12 reports, citing multiple unidentified sources.

Right now, the only real obstacle standing in Netanyahu's way is the IDF Chief of Staff, Lieutenant General Eyal Zamir, who once again opposed these moves in closed meetings.

Zamir's assessment is purely pragmatic: an offensive now is imprudent given the internal political uncertainty and the lack of American backing.

The NYT report added that Zamir had "became concerned" that Netanyahu was "pushing for a separate action against Iran, short of an overt strike," which the IDF chief believed "would be a miscalculation that could provoke Iran into attacking Israel."

However, Channel 13 reported that the military assessment presented at the meeting indicated there was no immediate need for an Israeli strike.

According to the Channel 13 report, Israel should join the fighting if the United States resumes attacks on Iran.

**Significance:** [REPORTED] The civil-military tension is now explicit and public. IDF Chief Zamir — the professional military commander responsible for operational planning — stands alone in opposing Netanyahu's push for military action, while all Netanyahu-appointed intelligence and political officials reportedly back strikes. This creates a potential constitutional crisis if Netanyahu attempts to override Zamir's professional judgment. The Sunday security cabinet meeting is the critical flashpoint where this tension will be tested.

## 4. IAEA: "Serious Proliferation Concern" — Iran Extends Inspector Blackout

The United Nations nuclear watchdog has urged Iran to allow it to resume inspections of its nuclear facilities. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) called for Tehran to restore access for its inspectors in a report circulated by the watchdog among member states on Friday.

"The Agency does not know [the Isfahan Fuel Enrichment Plant's] exact location, status for safeguards purposes or whether it contains nuclear material," the IAEA said in a report to member states.

The IAEA said a substantial portion of Iran's most highly enriched uranium is likely stored inside an underground tunnel complex at the Isfahan nuclear site. He said Iran has blocked inspectors at Isfahan since June 2025, leaving its stockpile of highly enriched uranium unverified.

The IAEA said the lack of information about Iran's atomic sites and nuclear material is "a matter of proliferation concern" that "needs to be rectified with the utmost urgency," The Associated Press news agency reported.

IAEA inspectors were able to visit Iran's Bushehr nuclear plant in September, but have had no access to other facilities.

Iran told the U.N. nuclear watchdog this month that discussions on nuclear monitoring cannot proceed while it is under threat from military attacks.

The IAEA said its board of governors is scheduled to meet next week in Vienna to discuss Iran's nuclear file.

**Significance:** [OFFICIAL] The IAEA report adds urgency to the nuclear dimension of the conflict. The watchdog's inability to verify the location or status of Iran's most sensitive enrichment facility creates significant uncertainty about actual Iranian capabilities. This could fuel both Israeli arguments for preemptive strikes and broader international concern. The IAEA Board of Governors meeting next week is a potential trigger for diplomatic or policy action.

## 5. Iran: No Uranium Transfer — "Not a Single Atom"

Iran on Wednesday ruled out transferring its highly enriched nuclear material out of the country as part of any peace agreement with the U.S., as diplomacy between the two countries faltered. Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, told Lebanese outlet Al Mayadeen TV that his country would not "remove a single atom of enriched nuclear material" and that Russia is aware of Tehran's position.

"We need enriched materials to design ships and submarines powered by nuclear energy," Rezaei told Lebanon's Al Mayadeen television network in an interview published on Nov. 10.

Iran had informed Russia of the decision, Rezaei noted. Previously, Russia has offered to take in and store the enriched uranium.

American officials had been exploring a package linking a ceasefire, partial sanctions relief and externalisation of enriched uranium, in order to reassure Congress and regional allies about the purely civil nature of Iran's programme.

**Significance:** [OFFICIAL] Combined with Ghalibaf's statement that Hormuz will "never return" to pre-war status, Iran is systematically rejecting every major U.S. negotiating demand. The uranium transfer was considered a potential key concession that could unlock a deal; its categorical rejection significantly narrows diplomatic pathways. Russia's offer to host the material has been explicitly rejected by Tehran, eliminating what appeared to be a face-saving compromise option.

Trump met Qatar's prime minister at the White House this week, and said afterward that Gulf Arab allies prefer "the status quo" of continued economic pressure on Iran. He wrote that while the U.S. remains "locked and loaded", an attack at this point "seems to be quite unlikely."

Trump then identified the countries that had asked him to exercise restraint.

"We're just keeping it the status quo," Trump said on Monday. That status quo still involves a fair amount of shooting. U.S. forces have hit air-defense sites on Iran's Qeshm Island at least twice this month.

So status quo means Iran keeps hitting ships in the Strait of Hormuz.

If, by status quo, the president means the circumstances of the past few months, then he'd be referring to a Strait of Hormuz that remains heavily restricted, intermittent attacks on oil tankers, the indefinite presence of American aircraft carriers and warships in the Middle East, and a naval blockade of Iran's ports.

**Significance:** [OFFICIAL] Trump is explicitly citing Gulf allies as his rationale for restraint, giving him political cover to resist Netanyahu's pressure while maintaining the blockade strategy. The "status quo" framing signals the administration has accepted indefinite stalemate as the preferred outcome — neither pursuing escalation nor diplomatic resolution. This aligns with yesterday's "no deal needed" declaration and confirms economic strangulation without resolution as the operative policy framework.

## 7. Maritime Attacks Intensify — LNG Tanker Reportedly Hits Mine

Iran's Revolutionary Guards navy said a tanker carrying liquefied natural gas struck a naval mine and suffered an explosion in the Strait of Hormuz on Friday while using what they described was an "unauthorized and dangerous route," state media reported.

The claim could not be independently verified, and there was no immediate report of the incident from the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations, a British military agency that monitors incidents at sea.

At least 10 ships were hit in the first 10 days of November, after 37 attacks in October, which was the highest monthly tally since the conflict began in late February, according to data from the Washington Institute for Near East Policy.

Tankers are still facing attacks across the strait, despite a rebound in traffic and expectations of a de-escalation in the US-Israeli war on Iran, that have been reflected in falling oil prices.

The tankers had ignored warnings from Iran and entered restricted areas of the Strait, Tehran said, as it remains defiant that it controls transits.

**Significance:** [UNCONFIRMED] Iran's claim of another mine strike remains unverified by UKMTO or other independent maritime authorities. However, the elevated pace of attacks — 10 ships in the first 10 days of November following 37 in October — demonstrates that the Hormuz chokepoint remains an active conflict zone regardless of falling oil prices and diplomatic expectations. Iran continues asserting control over transit, requiring vessels to seek Iranian approval.

## 8. Turkey-Qatar Defense Pact — Regional Security Architecture Shifting

Turkey and Qatar signed a mutual defence agreement on Friday aimed at strengthening collective defence and promoting regional security, and pledged a unified stance against any external armed attack targeting either country, according to a joint statement issued after talks between their leaders.

In August, Turkey, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan signed a joint defence agreement in Mecca, bringing together the Sunni Muslim US allies at a time of conflict across the region. Turkey's parliament ratified the so-called Mecca Joint Defence Agreement last week, clearing the way for Ankara to send military support to Saudi Arabia, which has been struggling to fend off attacks by Yemen's Iran-backed Houthi militants.

Turkey and Qatar would continue working together "to prevent a renewed outbreak of war in the Gulf region and to promote dialogue and peace", Erdogan said.

The Turkish troops in Qatar are very important but I can say that in the near term Turkish weapons and air defences might be deployed to Qatar to guarantee its safety, which is very important to Turkiye, so this agreement will help move forward these military deals," Alabarda said.

**Significance:** [VERIFIED] The Turkey-Qatar pact follows August's Mecca Joint Defence Agreement between Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan. This proliferation of mutual defense commitments represents Gulf states hedging against continued regional instability and uncertainty about U.S. commitment. Turkey's potential deployment of air defenses to Qatar would significantly alter the regional security landscape.
