## Critical Developments (September 15)

### 1. CRITICAL: Pentagon Inspector General Confirms Munitions Shortfalls [VERIFIED]

[VERIFIED] The US fired so many munitions at Iranian targets during the first few months of Operation Epic Fury that it "has resulted in strategic inventory shortfalls and revealed industrial base bottlenecks for munitions resupply," Pentagon officials acknowledged to the Defense Department's independent watchdog. The IG report, which covers the US military's operations in the conflict with Iran through June 30, marks an official confirmation of a problem the Trump administration has repeatedly downplayed.

[VERIFIED] The report says the war with Iran has cost roughly $33.4 billion, which does not account for repairing damaged facilities. It includes $22.3 billion in expended munitions, $3.7 billion for equipment losses, and $7.4 billion in other expenditures. NBC News reported in July that the war had so far cost $80 billion to $100 billion, according to three U.S. officials.

[REPORTED] The Pentagon's first mandatory report on the war with Iran revealed that the U.S. military has consumed 80% of its THAAD missile interceptors and half of its Patriot missiles since the conflict began in February 2026.

[OFFICIAL] Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has denied reports that the U.S. is running low on munitions. President Donald Trump said Monday on Truth Social that the U.S. is "producing more Exquisite and Elite Weapons than at any time in our History."

**Assessment:** This is a significant development. The Pentagon IG's acknowledgment of "strategic inventory shortfalls" directly contradicts administration messaging. The 80% THAAD and 50% Patriot depletion figures, if accurate, represent serious capability constraints for sustained operations or a potential second-front contingency. This could affect Saudi confidence in U.S. extended deterrence and may factor into Riyadh's calculations about unilateral action.

### 2. CRITICAL: Iraq Militia Disarmament Deadline Nears — Compliance Unlikely [VERIFIED]

[VERIFIED] Two deadlines are looming for Iraq at the end of the month: the end of a decades-long U.S. military presence and the disarmament of non-state armed groups. The U.S. withdrawal is underway, and officials say the hundreds of troops remaining in northern Iraq will be out by the Sept. 30 deadline. The Iraqi government has linked the U.S. withdrawal to an ultimatum for militias to lay down their weapons. But most have indicated they have no plans to do so.

[VERIFIED] "There will be no disarming of any groups by the 30th of September," said Iraqi analyst Sajad Jiyad, speaking at a panel convened by the Atlantic Center think tank last week. "I think that's probably clear to anybody who watches Iraqi politics." The most powerful groups — and closest to Iran — Kataib Hezbollah, Harakat al-Nujaba and Kataib Sayyid al-Shuhada have rejected the prospect or set conditions for disarming.

[VERIFIED] Iranian-backed Iraqi militias have struck US-linked targets inside and outside Iraq since the US and Israel attacked Iran on February 28. They also have coordinated with Yemen's Houthi rebels to attack Saudi Arabia. With the war between the US and Iran raging, Tehran is unlikely to give up the leverage it has in neighbouring Iraq.

[REPORTED] A U.S. military official said the withdrawal would be completed by Sept. 30, and the hundreds of troops still present in northern Iraq would mostly be redeployed to Jordan and other countries in the region. Military equipment, including air defense systems, will also be removed.

**Assessment:** This creates a significant near-term inflection point. The Sept 30 deadline will remove U.S. air defenses from northern Iraq/Kurdistan while the militias that launched the East-West Pipeline attacks remain armed. This structurally increases the risk of repeat attacks on Saudi infrastructure from Iraqi territory while reducing U.S. deterrence capability.

### 3. MAJOR: Human Rights Watch Declares Houthi Shipping Attacks Likely War Crimes [OFFICIAL]

[OFFICIAL] Human Rights Watch stated that the Houthis' latest attacks on commercial vessels in the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden most likely amount to war crimes in at least three cases. The attacks follow the Houthis' announcement on July 20, 2026, that they would impose a maritime embargo on Saudi Arabia. The new attacks targeted at least two Saudi-flagged vessels, a Tanzania-flagged vessel, and a Dominica-flagged vessel.

[OFFICIAL] "The Houthis are once again putting seafarers' lives at deadly risk through their attacks on commercial ships, this time as part of their hostilities with the Saudi-led coalition," said Niku Jafarnia, Yemen and Bahrain researcher at Human Rights Watch. "Whatever the geopolitics, attacks on civilian crews carried out deliberately or recklessly are war crimes."

[REPORTED] As of August 24, the Houthis claimed that they had targeted at least nine vessels since July 20.

**Assessment:** The HRW designation provides potential legal/diplomatic cover for expanded international action against Houthi maritime operations. However, absent enforcement mechanisms, this is more significant for diplomatic framing than operational impact. It may increase pressure on China to condition its Houthi back-channel arrangement on reduced attacks.

**Net assessment:** The strategic picture has become more complex with the confirmation of U.S. capability constraints. Combined with the imminent U.S. withdrawal from Iraq — which removes deterrent capability while Iranian-backed militias remain armed — this creates a deteriorating capability-to-commitment ratio.

With 49 days to midterms, oil above $107, and no de-escalation pathway, the conflict has settled into a structural impairment phase that will define the economic and political landscape through November 3.
