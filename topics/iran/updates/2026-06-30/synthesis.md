## Critical Developments (June 30)

### 1. CRITICAL: Doha Talks — Both Sides Present But Not Meeting Directly [VERIFIED]

The arrival of Trump's son-in-law Jared Kushner and envoy Steve Witkoff in Doha on Tuesday followed exchanges of fire over the weekend that tested the June 17 interim accord between the United States and Iran.

Jared Kushner is expected to meet with Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman bin Jassim Al Thani and other mediators in Doha today.

[OFFICIAL] While Iran is sending its technical delegation to Qatar this week, Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baghaei said this had "no relation" to the Americans' visit and no talks between the two sides were scheduled. "We will not have any negotiation meetings at any level with the American side in the coming days," Baghaei said.

[OFFICIAL] Trump told reporters in the Oval Office, "the meeting in Doha is going to be perhaps important, perhaps not. We're going to find out." At the same time he maintained "we're winning militarily" and repeated his condition that Iran must be stopped from producing a nuclear weapon.

[REPORTED] Regardless of how Iran describes the visit, the simultaneous presence of expert delegations from Tehran and Washington in Doha following the weekend's tensions is significant and cannot be overlooked. Iranian negotiators' attempts to distance themselves from any talks may also be a tactic to ease domestic pressure from hardline factions. Framing the visit as focused on securing the release of funds — rather than negotiations — could be another way to relieve that pressure.

[REPORTED] According to analyst Seloom, the planned talks in Doha "aren't a breakthrough but damage control." "The weekend Hormuz strikes shrank a nuclear round into one clause. Tehran's public denial isn't refusal. It's leverage," he said.

**Assessment:** The Doha dynamic confirms the diplomatic track has shifted to crisis management. Both sides are physically present but Iran denies direct contact, framing its delegation as focused on frozen assets and MOU implementation. This is either genuine internal pressure from Iranian hardliners or deliberate leverage. Either way, comprehensive deal-making has given way to narrow implementation disputes.

### 2. CRITICAL: Hormuz Security Threat Raised to "Substantial" — Mines Persist [VERIFIED]

Even as commercial traffic remained steady, the security threat level for the critical Strait of Hormuz has been raised to substantial due to "additional mine risk persisting and clearance operations ongoing." The JMIC also said navigation interference, hailing by Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, drone activity and surveillance all continued in the area.

No confirmed maritime security incidents were listed in the latest reporting period. The US-led JMIC is a multinational naval advisory body based in Bahrain.

[OFFICIAL] Safely evacuating more than 8,500 seafarers still stuck in the Strait of Hormuz requires Iranian guarantees that no vessels will be targeted, the head of the UN's International Maritime Organization (IMO) said Tuesday. "Once the reassurance comes back that no vessel that is being evacuated will be targeted, we are ready to immediately react," Arsenio Dominguez, the Secretary-General of the IMO, told CNN.

Dominguez also highlighted the importance of urgently demining the strait in order to allow a gradual return to vessels flowing more normally through the vital waterway. He commended countries, including France, for offering to assist in the demining process. However, Iran's deputy foreign minister has said the country will remove all of the mines by itself.

Effectively, the strait is closed to commercial shipping as of June 30, 2026. Only 5 ships transited vs ~93/day normal.

**Assessment:** The elevated threat level and mine persistence create a structural bottleneck for Hormuz normalization. Iran's insistence on conducting demining alone, while rejecting international assistance, means reopening depends entirely on Iranian timelines and willingness. The IMO cannot resume evacuations without Iranian guarantees — guarantees Iran has not provided since Thursday's ship attack. This is the core implementation failure of the MOU.

### 3. NEW: Iran Asserts Hormuz Control; Oman Discussing "Service Fees" [VERIFIED]

Tehran maintained that it intends to continue overseeing traffic through the Strait of Hormuz even if Oman opts not to participate. Under the current interim agreement, Iran will not impose transit fees for 60 days, though it leaves open the possibility of introducing charges afterward, a proposal opposed by the US, Europe, and Gulf Arab states.

[REPORTED] Iranian officials expressed a desire to co-regulate the strategic channel with Oman, but affirmed that Tehran will advance its own regulatory plans independently if necessary to oversee the strait's picking up flows.

[OFFICIAL] On Sunday, Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the MoU gives Tehran control of the waterway. The Strait of Hormuz would remain under Iranian control for 30 days, he said.

**Assessment:** Iran continues to assert "sole management" of Hormuz, viewing the 60-day toll suspension as temporary. The emerging Iran-Oman discussions on "service fees" represent a potential shift in Oman's position — previously aligned with US/IMO framework, now potentially moving toward co-management with Iran. If Oman shifts, the US-backed southern corridor loses its foundation.

### 4. Oil Prices at Pre-War Levels Despite Unresolved Crisis [VERIFIED]

As of 9 a.m. Eastern Time today, oil is trading at $75.02 per barrel, based on the Brent benchmark. That's $1.02 above yesterday morning's level and about $7.24 higher than where it stood a year ago.

International benchmark Brent crude futures with August delivery were last declining 0.2% to $72.99 per barrel. The contract is on track to end June roughly $19 lower, or 20% lower than the closing session on May 29.

Oil prices were on track Tuesday for their steepest quarterly decline since the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic. U.S. West Texas Intermediate (WTI) was headed for a second monthly decline of roughly 19%, while Brent crude was on pace for a third consecutive monthly drop, down about 20% in June. Both Brent and WTI have fallen sharply this quarter and are trading near pre-war levels as more ships move out of the Gulf.

[REPORTED] Energy analysts say they have been surprised by the pace of the sell-off in the oil market, noting that it has been far more aggressive than most had expected. "The price action in recent weeks reflects a market that is treating this temporary ceasefire between the US and Iran as a permanent deal. This is clearly not the case, and as we have seen over the last four months, the situation can change very quickly," strategists at ING said.

### 5. Shipping Traffic: Container Ships Returning, But Overall Flow Minimal [VERIFIED]

After the US and Iran traded new strikes over the weekend, shipping traffic in the crucial Strait of Hormuz has remained elevated and seems at least partially unfazed by the stop-and-start peace talks. Independent traffic service MarineTraffic reported Monday that three container vessels entered the Persian Gulf over the weekend, a significant sign of confidence as they represent the first commercial container ships to make the journey in that direction since the start of the conflict. One hundred and eight verified crossings were recorded in total from June 26-28.

Still, shipping traffic continued, though it remains well below the 100+ ships that crossed the strait daily prior to the war. The data released Monday from MarineTraffic showed that shippers have been utilizing multiple routes and successfully crossing along both Oman's and Iran's coastlines.

[OFFICIAL] National Economic Council director Kevin Hassett predicted on CNBC on Monday that traffic will soon be "really moving [and] back to more than 100 ships a day," which he added will ease price pressures around the world.

Crisis Pressure holds at 90 (extreme) while the 30-day Escalation Forecast ticks up to 69 (high), driven by Polymarket closure odds. Brent crude holds at $74.06; 485 vessels remain anchored or stopped in the region.

**Assessment:** The return of container ships is a significant confidence indicator — container lines are more risk-averse than tankers. However, overall traffic remains at ~5% of pre-war levels. The 485 vessels anchored in the region represent the stranded fleet that cannot move until the IMO receives Iranian guarantees. White House optimism about imminent normalization appears premature.

### 6. Israel-Lebanon Framework: Analysts Call It "Born Dead" [REPORTED]

As widely expected, Hezbollah rejected the framework outright. In a statement on Saturday, the group's Secretary-General Naim Qassem called the Washington agreement "null and void" and insisted that the Iran-US MoU should be the basis for ending the conflict. He also warned against linking Israel's withdrawal from Lebanon to Hezbollah's disarmament, saying that crossed "all red lines".

Ahron Bregman, a senior teaching fellow in the Department of War Studies at King's College London, described the framework as an Israeli-US attempt "to drive a wedge between the Lebanese and Iranian fronts" and to curtail "Iranian influence in Lebanon." He added: "It won't work, not in a million years".

The framework's main provisions are: Mutual recognition by each state of the other's sovereignty; gradual Israeli withdrawal from pilot areas in south Lebanon; the Lebanese army will enter to dismantle all non-state armed groups. The IDF withdrawal will be completed only after the dismantling of the groups and their infrastructure has been officially verified; Hezbollah and its allies rejected the framework agreement outright, claiming it was "surrender," undermined Lebanon's sovereignty and could lead to civil war.

[OFFICIAL] Defense Minister Israel Katz said Trump insisted on linking the wars in Lebanon and Iran during ceasefire talks, despite his country's desire to handle them as separate conflicts. He said Israel had received US backing to stay in Lebanon until Hezbollah is disarmed across the country.

Market pricing suggests the likelihood of a permanent peace deal between Israel and Hezbollah by June 30, 2026, has decreased significantly, with pricing at 1.4% YES, down from 3% 24 hours ago.

**Assessment:** The Israel-Lebanon framework contains the same structural contradiction identified in prior reports: Israel conditions withdrawal on Hezbollah disarmament; Hezbollah refuses to disarm; Lebanon's government cannot enforce disarmament. The framework formalizes stalemate rather than resolving conflict. Israel has US backing for indefinite presence, while Hezbollah maintains that the US-Iran MOU — not this framework — should govern Lebanon's future.

### 7. Frozen Assets: Conflicting Claims Continue [REPORTED]

[OFFICIAL] Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian said Iran will honor its commitments if the US does the same, while warning that Tehran will respond firmly to threats. He also said that half of Iran's $12 billion in frozen assets held in Qatar will be returned to Tehran — an issue on which the US has issued conflicting statements.

Qatar remains in possession of the $6 billion of Iranian frozen funds, according to US officials. Doha has been coordinating with Oman on the safe passage for vessels.

[OFFICIAL] Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesperson Baghaei said the United States has issued the necessary licenses tied to Clause 10, which covers oil sales, and that Iran is following up on implementing them. He said the implementation of Clause 11, related to the release of Iran's frozen assets, is also being pursued.

**Assessment:** The frozen assets dispute exemplifies the fundamental disconnect. Iran claims $6B will be released; the US says nothing has been transferred; Qatar has not confirmed either position. Each side accuses the other of non-compliance. This dispute is likely driving Iran's refusal to engage in direct talks — from Tehran's perspective, the US hasn't fulfilled MOU commitments, so why negotiate further?

China's Foreign Minister Wang Yi called Tuesday for continued diplomatic momentum between the U.S. and Iran, emphasizing that negotiations remain the best path to easing tensions in the Middle East. "Talking is better than fighting, and dialog is better than confrontation," Wang warned, speaking in Beijing during a meeting with his Saudi counterpart.

**Assessment:** China's statement is notable given its Security Council veto of the Bahrain-led maritime security resolution in April. Beijing prefers managed diplomacy to US military enforcement — consistent with China receiving a third of its oil via the strait. Chinese pressure on Iran to continue talks may be a stabilizing factor.

## Emerging Signals

**Indirect mediation as new normal:** The Doha format — both sides present but not meeting directly, with Qatar as go-between — may become the default diplomatic posture. This can prevent collapse but is unlikely to produce fast, enforceable outcomes. The shift from Switzerland (nuclear focus) to Doha (crisis management) signals narrowed ambitions.

If Iran delays or fails to clear mines, Hormuz cannot normalize regardless of diplomatic progress.

**Israel-Lebanon framework formalizing stalemate:** The framework may serve to provide "political cover" for Israel's indefinite presence in Lebanon while creating the appearance of progress.
