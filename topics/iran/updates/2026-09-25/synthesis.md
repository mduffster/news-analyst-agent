# US-Israel-Iran Conflict: Iran's Seven-Day Proposal Sharpens Diplomatic Focus

## September 25, 2026

The diplomatic track crystallized significantly today with Iran publicly articulating a compressed seven-day timeline to reopen the Strait of Hormuz—a concrete proposal that compresses the failed June MoU framework into an accelerated implementation window. Foreign Minister Araghchi detailed the terms at UNGA: within seven days, the US would lift its naval blockade, release frozen Iranian assets (approximately $12 billion), waive oil sanctions, and observe a regionwide ceasefire including Lebanon; on the seventh day, Hormuz would reopen, followed immediately by comprehensive nuclear negotiations. The proposal represents a significant procedural advance even as the fundamental sequencing dispute persists. Oil prices fell approximately 2% on Friday amid diplomatic optimism, while fighting around Bab al-Mandab intensified with over 150 killed in 72 hours. The Trump-Xi summit concluded without visible deliverables on Iran, and a Washington Post investigation revealed US military fatalities significantly exceed Pentagon's public disclosure.

## Iran's Seven-Day Proposal: Compressed Timeline, Unchanged Preconditions

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi told reporters at a press briefing at the UN General Assembly that Tehran had proposed to Washington a seven-day plan to cease hostilities, reopen the Strait of Hormuz and then begin comprehensive talks on Iran's nuclear program. [REPORTED]

Under the conditions Iran has set, during the seven days all hostilities would end, including in Lebanon; the US would release Iran's frozen assets, estimated at least $12 billion; waive sanctions on Iranian oil; and lift the naval blockade on Iran. Then, on the last day, the Strait of Hormuz would be opened. After the seven days, comprehensive negotiations over Iran's nuclear program would immediately begin. [REPORTED]

"If certain conditions are met, the Strait of Hormuz will be open at the end of seven days, and talks will be restarted," Araghchi told reporters. "The conditions we have asked the U.S. to meet are nothing new, nothing more than what was already in the Islamabad MOU, which was signed by the U.S. president." [OFFICIAL]

The Trump administration has previously said it will not return to the terms of the June agreement. [REPORTED]

**Critical assessment:** The proposal closely resembles the MoU, but sets out a hugely ambitious timetable. The Pakistani and Qatari-brokered deal signed on June 17 set a 60-day window for negotiations, but still unraveled within weeks. Vague wording led to disputes over who controlled shipping through the strait, and the two sides traded attacks before President Trump declared the deal "over" on July 7.

It's not clear if the proposal would include the revived conflict between Yemen's Iran-backed Houthi rebels and Saudi Arabia. Iran appears to think the Houthis' newly enhanced ability to threaten the Bab el-Mandeb strait could push Trump to make a deal. This omission is significant—any agreement that does not address Yemen risks being undermined by continued Houthi operations against Saudi Red Sea infrastructure.

Araghchi detailed the proposal in a private gathering on Thursday on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly. Trita Parsi, executive vice president of the Quincy Institute, who attended, said Araghchi suggested that a speedy agreement would help President Trump ahead of November's midterm elections. This framing positions the proposal as mutually advantageous politically, though it also reveals Tehran's awareness of US electoral constraints.

## Trump-Xi Summit: Iran Discussed, No Visible Progress

President Trump and Chinese leader Xi Jinping met Thursday afternoon for around three hours at the White House during the first day of a two-day summit between the leaders of the world's two largest economies. [VERIFIED]

Despite the pageantry and the cordial tone, expectations for any big breakthroughs on issues ranging from AI to Iran to a trade war truce were low. [REPORTED]

Xi's state visit comes as the US continues a months-long naval blockade on Iranian ports in the Strait of Hormuz, cutting off an estimated 12% of China's oil imports. China is Iran's largest trading partner, and Trump may ask Xi to play a part in peace talks with Tehran, analysts say. China's role remains the underexplored variable—Beijing has leverage with Tehran but has not publicly deployed it.

A senior US official told PBS that Americans have died in Iranian attacks in the Middle East as a result of Chinese actions. The US would like to see those actions, reportedly targeting assistance, stopped. [REPORTED] This accusation—the first public attribution linking Chinese support to US casualties—could complicate any Chinese mediation role.

## Oil Markets: Prices Decline on Diplomatic Hopes

At 9 a.m. Eastern Time today, oil was priced at $102.75 per barrel with Brent serving as the benchmark. That's a drop of $1.92 compared with yesterday morning and around $33 higher than the price one year ago. [VERIFIED]

Reuters reported that Brent crude was down $1.47, or 1.4%, at $105.11 a barrel, while West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude fell $2, or 2.1%, to $92.61 a barrel at 0541 GMT. [REPORTED]

Crude Oil fell to 92.55 USD/Bbl (WTI) on September 25, 2026, down 2.18% from the previous day. [VERIFIED]

The market is treating the seven-day proposal as a potential de-escalation signal, though price variation across sources reflects ongoing volatility and the conflation of different benchmarks and observation times. One of the main factors behind Friday's decline is the possibility of progress toward a US-Iran truce.

## Hormuz Shipping: Severely Depressed Despite US Claims of Progress

Conflicting data complicates the picture. Effectively, the Strait of Hormuz is closed to commercial shipping as of September 25, 2026. Convoys are moving under naval escort. One ship transited on September 20 versus approximately 85 per day normal. [REPORTED]

However, Admiral Brad Cooper, head of US Central Command, said in a video message on September 19 that oil and liquefied natural gas shipments through the Strait of Hormuz in the past two weeks reached the highest level in six months, signaling that US naval protection and mine clearance efforts are "paying off." [OFFICIAL]

The strait's primary transit lanes are clear of mines, and Persian Gulf allies have shipped more than 1 billion barrels of crude through the waterway "in the last couple months." [OFFICIAL]

Meanwhile, Iran "has exported zero barrels" as the US enforces a blockade in the strait. [OFFICIAL]

These official US claims and independent ship-tracking data present sharply divergent pictures. The 1 billion barrels over "months" is compatible with severely reduced daily flows; pre-war the strait handled roughly 21 million barrels daily. The key takeaway: some escorted transit is occurring, but nowhere near pre-war commercial volumes.

## Yemen/Bab al-Mandab: Deadliest Fighting Since Civil War Resumed

Houthi and Yemeni government forces clashed over coastal highlands near al-Jawf, Marib, Saada, and Taiz, killing at least 154 people over the past three days. [REPORTED]

By mid-September, the Houthis' rapid advance had brought them to within about 20 miles of the US military base in Djibouti. As of mid-September 2026, Houthi forces control Yemen's entire Red Sea coastline, Perim Island, and the Hanish Islands, giving them strategic control over the Bab al-Mandab Strait. [VERIFIED]

The strait's strategic importance had increased dramatically because Iran's closure of the Strait of Hormuz amid the 2026 Iran war had forced Saudi Arabia to rely far more heavily on its Red Sea export route; between March and mid-July 2026, Saudi seaborne crude exports through Bab al-Mandab were eight times higher than during the same period in 2025. [VERIFIED]

At UNGA, Yemen's Vice President Abdullah al-Alimi warned that the Houthis were using shipping security "as a tool for blackmail." "It is taking hostage the entire global economy," he said. [OFFICIAL]

Iranian Maj. Gen. Yahya Rahim Safavi, military adviser to Iran's supreme leader, told state television that the Americans "face a new problem called the Bab el-Mandeb Strait." He added: "I think the process that is unfolding is in our favor, and the Americans will be forced to accept Iran's conditions." [REPORTED]

The Bab al-Mandab fighting represents a second front in the energy chokepoint conflict. With Hormuz under blockade and Bab al-Mandab increasingly contested, Saudi Arabia's export options have narrowed to its East-West pipeline to Yanbu—which Houthis attacked on September 24. Iran's seven-day proposal notably does not address Yemen.

## US Casualties: Pentagon Figures Disputed

More American service members have died in the Middle East during the ongoing Iran war than the Pentagon has disclosed publicly, according to six US officials familiar with the Defense Department's internal casualty accounting data. [REPORTED] (Washington Post)

The Pentagon's disclosed total stands at 19 fatalities, though US officials say the actual number is higher. The Pentagon has added one death to its public database, bringing the disclosed total to 19. [REPORTED]

Another US soldier died this month, according to newly released Pentagon statistics that classify the death as "non-hostile." An additional 66 US personnel were wounded in action this month, pushing the official count of Iran war casualties above 850. [REPORTED]

Defense Secretary Hegseth has called the Washington Post's report "false." The discrepancy between disclosed (19) and reported (22-24) fatalities creates a credibility gap that could fuel domestic political opposition to the war.

On September 9, 2026, the IAEA Board of Governors adopted a resolution urging Iran to cooperate with the Agency. [OFFICIAL]

At UNGA, Pezeshkian emphasized double standards: "The atomic bomb and other weapons of mass destruction in this region are in the hands of the Israeli regime; yet inspections are demanded of Iran!" While rhetorically striking, this does not constitute a new commitment to inspections—it is a deflection framed as criticism of Western policy.

## Watchlist

**US response to seven-day proposal:** Whether Washington engages with specific terms, proposes counter-sequencing, or restates rejection of June terms. Secretary Rubio's statement that the US would "exchange ideas and messages" suggests engagement remains possible. Look for any official response within the next 48-72 hours.

**Talks continuation at UNGA:** Whether Araghchi-Witkoff technical discussions continue before Iranian delegation departs New York. Reports that talks have entered a "technical phase" with experts joining suggest deeper engagement, but neither side has announced a follow-up session.

**Houthi operations against Yanbu and East-West pipeline:** Thursday's missile attack on Yanbu came two days after pipeline restart. Whether this becomes sustained campaign or isolated demonstration will determine viability of Saudi's alternative export route. War-risk premiums for Saudi-linked tankers at Yanbu have tripled since early July.
