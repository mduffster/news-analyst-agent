### 1. CRITICAL: Doha Technical Talks Underway — But Only Indirect Format [VERIFIED]

President Trump said there was progress Wednesday as U.S. and Iranian officials resumed talks in Qatar, via mediators, telling reporters "the denuclearization of Iran is moving along well." Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, President Trump's son-in-law, were in Doha amid the new round of indirect talks, and they met with Qatar's prime minister but were not expected to take part in the technical negotiations.

Sources tell Al Jazeera that "technical level" talks are now under way in Qatar's capital Doha but senior US-Iranian negotiators will not meet face to face.

[OFFICIAL] Tehran has denied holding direct talks with the US in Doha, and many Iranian leaders are not present. Instead, Iran said it will hold indirect talks on the US-Iran deal with mediator Qatar to discuss implementing the MoU with the US and releasing frozen Iranian assets.

[OFFICIAL] Trump's special envoy Steve Witkoff and his son-in-law Jared Kushner were in Doha today with Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman bin Jassim Al Thani and other mediators. Delegations from the US and Iran are expected to participate separately tomorrow in technical talks with mediators from Qatar and Pakistan.

**Assessment:** The Doha format confirms the diplomatic track has narrowed to indirect, mediated crisis management. Pakistan's emergence as a co-mediator alongside Qatar represents a widening of the mediation architecture. Trump's characterization of "very good meetings" contrasts with the structural limitations: no direct contact, technical-level only, and Iran framing participation as MOU implementation rather than new negotiations.

### 2. CRITICAL: Trump Rejects "Finishing the Job" War Option [REPORTED]

[REPORTED] According to the Wall Street Journal, Trump was briefed on full-scale war options described by some aides as "finishing the job" but decided against it, telling advisers that another round of heavy strikes could derail negotiations and jeopardize the broader goal of dismantling Iran's nuclear program entirely.

[REPORTED] The Wall Street Journal has reported that Trump, when presented recently with a full range of options to restart the war on Iran, told his advisers that he preferred to give diplomacy a chance, and was willing to extend the 60-day truce if needed for discussions on Iran's nuclear program.

[OFFICIAL] US Vice President JD Vance told Fox News that the Trump administration was in a "great position" regardless of how the talks pan out. The US "obviously" wants the talks to succeed, Vance said, but added that his country was "still in a much stronger position" than Iran even if they fail.

**Assessment:** This is a significant revelation about US decision-making calculus. Trump's willingness to extend past August 18 removes that date as a hard escalation trigger. The administration is prioritizing a durable deal over timeline pressure, shifting the dynamic from "countdown to potential escalation" to "extended uncertainty." However, the WSJ editorial board criticism that Iran doesn't believe Trump's "social-media bluster because they see his reluctance to enforce the cease-fire terms" captures the risk: Iran may interpret this as weakness rather than strategic patience.

### 3. CRITICAL: Ship Grounding — Iran Reinforces Route Control Claims [REPORTED]

A foreign container ship ran aground in the shallow waters on the Omani side of the Strait of Hormuz on Wednesday, as Tehran continues to insist on maintaining control of the key waterway, according to Iranian state television and multiple Iranian official sources. Iran's IRGC claimed the grounding was linked to the vessel not using Iran's approved route. Iranian officials said they had "repeatedly warned captains, shipowners and officials of shipping companies around the world that any entry or exit through routes other than the 'Route of Authority' in the Persian Gulf could lead to irreparable incidents."

The vessel is believed to have been using the Oman corridor established last week by Muscat in coordination with the International Maritime Organization as an alternative to Iran's designated route south of Larak Island.

"The IRGC Navy continuously warns captains, owners, and officials of shipping companies around the world that any entry or exit through routes other than the designated route under Iran's authority in the Persian Gulf may result in irreparable incidents." Iran's navy also warned vessels against using routes outside those designated by the newly created Persian Gulf Strait Authority (PGSA).

**Assessment:** Even if this is a navigational accident rather than coercion, Iran is using it operationally and politically to reinforce its "approved corridor" claims. The vessel was reportedly using the IMO/Oman-backed southern corridor — meaning Iran is effectively warning ships away from the alternative route. This creates a binary choice for shipping: comply with Iran's designated route (and implicitly acknowledge Iranian authority) or risk "irreparable incidents." This increases the risk that future "noncompliant" transits could trigger interception or escalation.

### 4. Iran Oil Export Surge — 40-50 Million Barrels at Premium Prices [VERIFIED]

Iran has exported more than 40 million barrels of crude oil since the U.S. removed its naval blockade of Iranian ports, and is now selling oil at prices roughly 20% higher than before the war, parliament speaker and chief negotiator Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said Tuesday. "Since the day the naval blockade was lifted, we have exported more than 40 million barrels of oil," Ghalibaf said in a television interview published on his Telegram channel. Iran had been unable to export a single barrel during the roughly two-month blockade that preceded the accord, he added.

Tanker tracking firm TankerTrackers.com said Wednesday that it estimated Iran had exported 50 million barrels of crude oil since the U.S. lifted its naval blockade on the country's energy exports two weeks ago. The firm uses satellite imagery, shoreside photography, and a real-time automatic identification system to monitor vessel movement.

Brent crude traded near $73 a barrel Wednesday, down almost 40% from the war's peak of $118 in April, as diplomatic progress and expectations of a Gulf supply rebound weigh on prices.

**Assessment:** Iran's rapid financial recovery undermines US leverage. Before the war, Iranian crude sold at a $10-15 discount to Brent due to sanctions risk; now Tehran claims a 20% premium. This validates Iran's strategy of leveraging Hormuz rather than fully reopening it — they're extracting maximum economic benefit while maintaining control claims. The 40-50M barrel surge in two weeks (roughly 2.5-3.5M barrels/day average) suggests Iran has prioritized export restoration over Hormuz normalization.

### 6. Israel Postpones Lebanon Pilot Zone Withdrawal [OFFICIAL]

Israel has decided Tuesday to postpone its withdrawal from two pilot areas in southern Lebanon, citing an alleged need to "wait until a joint monitoring mechanism is established with Beirut." "During discussions within the Israeli army regarding the plan, which Lebanon and Israel agreed upon during weekend talks, it was concluded that the implementation timeline would be delayed beyond the initial estimate," Israel's public broadcaster KAN quoted unnamed sources. "The withdrawal of Israeli forces from the two Lebanese areas of Zawtar and Frun (in the south) will be delayed," the sources added.

Israeli officials have told Lebanon that leaving the two designated "pilot zones" occupied by their troops will take time, a Lebanese military official said, in what would delay the implementation of an agreement between the two countries. "We are waiting for them. They said it would take time."

The mayor of a southern Lebanese village has questioned Israel's reported withdrawal plan. In an interview with RT, Hassan Adel Bazzi said that the community of Froun was designated as a pilot withdrawal zone despite never being occupied by Israeli forces. The mayor stressed that "Froun is located outside the yellow line," referring to Israel's self-declared buffer zone in southern Lebanon. "And we know that an experimental zone must be occupied territory," Bazzi said.

Regional analyst and former Israeli military intelligence officer Danny Citrinowicz told Reuters the dismantling of Hezbollah is "something that would ‌never happen," ⁠and the deal ultimately legitimizes an indefinite Israeli military presence in Lebanon. He said any Israeli prime minister is unlikely to halt the fight against Hezbollah while the group still poses a threat, so "nothing will happen. Israel won't withdraw, and Hezbollah won't dismantle."

**Assessment:** The Israel-Lebanon framework is struggling on day one of implementation. The postponement of pilot zone withdrawal — plus the revelation that Froun was never occupied by Israeli forces — suggests the "pilot zone" concept may be more about political optics than actual territorial transfer. The structural problem identified in prior reports is playing out: Israel conditions withdrawal on Hezbollah disarmament; Hezbollah refuses to disarm; the framework formalizes stalemate rather than producing outcomes.

### 7. Ghalibaf: Hormuz Is Iran's "Greatest Instrument of Power" [OFFICIAL]

[OFFICIAL] Iran's leading negotiator, Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, said Tehran would not enter talks on a final agreement with the US until Washington began implementing key parts of the June 17 interim deal. He cited Clause 13, which conditions the start of final negotiations on progress on earlier provisions.

[OFFICIAL] In a television interview, Ghalibaf described the strait as Iran's "greatest instrument of power" and insisted Tehran will retain control over the waterway's administration.

Under the interim memorandum of understanding signed on 17 June, ships may transit the waterway without fees for 60 days. Tehran insists it must control the route taken by vessels and intends to charge transit fees once that period expires — a position Washington and Gulf Arab states have flatly rejected.

A senior US official told AP that the Hormuz dispute and the situation in Lebanon remain the two main obstacles to a final agreement.

**Assessment:** Ghalibaf's framing is revealing: Iran views Hormuz control as leverage, not a concession to be traded. The insistence that final talks cannot begin until MOU provisions are implemented creates a chicken-and-egg standoff. Iran claims the US hasn't fulfilled commitments (frozen assets, Clause 10/11); the US claims Iran hasn't normalized Hormuz. This structural impasse explains why the MOU is stalling rather than progressing.

### 8. Frozen Assets: Qatar Confirms No Transfer Yet [OFFICIAL]

[OFFICIAL] Qatar's Foreign Ministry spokesman, Majed Al Ansari, said earlier that about $6 billion in Iranian assets held in Qatar had yet to be transferred to Tehran, underscoring one of the most sensitive elements of the arrangement.

This week, Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian said Iran expected $6bn of the frozen Iranian funds to be released by the US as a first step.

[OFFICIAL] Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesman Baghaei said discussions with Qatar would review how the interim US-Iran deal was being carried out and that steps to release Iran's frozen funds were under way, while emphasizing that key financial provisions remained unresolved.

**Assessment:** Qatar's confirmation that the $6B has not been transferred is significant. This is likely the core issue driving Iran's refusal to engage in direct talks — from Tehran's perspective, the US hasn't fulfilled its MOU commitments. The frozen assets dispute exemplifies the fundamental disconnect between the two sides' understanding of what the MOU requires.

### 9. Hormuz Governance Dispute: Iran-Oman Toll Proposal Circulating [REPORTED]

Iran's position — that it holds sovereign authority over the waterway and may designate approved routes and eventually charge transit fees — directly contradicts longstanding international law and the position of the US, the Gulf states and most of the international community, who regard the strait as an international waterway under the transit passage provisions of the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS). Iran has signed but never ratified it.

Qatar also insist that it "doesn't accept any change to the current status of the Strait of Hormuz, whether through unilateral action or otherwise," that "Qatar's position is based on respect for international maritime law, which guarantees freedom of navigation through the strait," and that "other Gulf and regional states, and the wider international community" share this principle.

[REPORTED] Iran and Oman have advanced a proposal for a "voluntary fee system" for ships transiting the strait, which has been circulated to Western governments. US Vice President JD Vance rejected the idea of Iranian-controlled charges on shipping, calling it unacceptable for Tehran to impose tolls on global trade routes.

**Assessment:** The Iran-Oman toll proposal represents a fait accompli strategy — establishing an alternative governance framework while the MOU remains in effect. If shipping companies begin paying, the precedent will be hard to reverse. Qatar's statement that it doesn't accept "any change to the current status" signals Gulf state resistance to Iranian-Omani co-management, but Oman's position appears to be shifting toward accommodation with Tehran.
